BusyPico

A toy with lights and switches

I am look to build a handheld, battery-powered game console based on the Raspberry Pi Pico 2. This will allow me to develop my understanding of electronics and programming.

The steps taken will be

  1. Prototype on breadboard
    Prototype on a BreadboardBefore soldering or mounting anything, you must verify the circuit. This is where you use the wiring we discussed earlier.
    • The Goal: Ensure the Pico 2 talks to the 74HC595, the potentiometer dims the LEDs, and the button triggers an action.
    • Check: Verify your 5 MHz SPI clock is stable and that the 2x AAA batteries power the system correctly.
  2. Prepare the Chassis
    Now you need a "home" for your console. You can use a 3D-printed case, a plastic project box, or even a sturdy piece of wood.
    • Drilling: Measure and drill holes for the Potentiometer (usually $7\text{mm}$ or $9\text{mm}$), the Button, and a slot for the 8 LEDs.
    • Mounting the Battery Holder: Use those M2/M2.5 flat-head screws we discussed to secure the battery tray inside or on the back of the case.
  3. Wiring and Soldering
    Once the components are mounted in the case, you'll replace the messy jumper wires with permanent connections.
    • Point-to-Point: Solder wires from the Pico 2 to the 74HC595 and the controls.
    • Power Rails: Create a "Power Bus" where the Battery (+), Pico VSYS, and 74HC595 VCC all meet.
    • Resistors: Don't forget to solder your $220\Omega$ resistors between the 74HC595 outputs and each LED.
  4. Software Architecture (The "Game Engine")
    Instead of one long script, organize your C code into a structure that makes it easy to add new games.
    • Drivers: Keep your SPI/Shift Register and ADC/PWM code in separate functions.
    • The Main Loop: Create a "Menu" state where you can use the Potentiometer to scroll through games and the Button to select one.
    • Battery Watchdog: Include your voltage monitoring code to blink a "Low Battery" LED if the voltage drops below $2.1\text{V}$.
  5. Game Development & Testing
    Now the fun part—coding the actual games!
    • Calibrate the Pot: Ensure the full turn of the knob maps perfectly to all 8 LEDs.
    • Refine the Feel: Adjust your software debouncing for the button so it feels "snappy" and responsive.
  6. Final Assembly and "Closing the Box"
    Now the fun part—coding the actual games!
    • Insulation: Use heat-shrink tubing or electrical tape on any exposed solder joints so they don't short-circuit against each other inside the case.
    • Strain Relief: Make sure the battery wires aren't being pulled tight when you close the lid.
    • The "Smoke Test": Power it on one last time with the batteries before screwing the case shut.
